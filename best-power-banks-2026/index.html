Best power banks 2026

High-wattage laptop charging, 15W Qi2 magnetic snap-on speed, and TSA-compliant battery packs for everyday travel.

There is nothing more anxiety-inducing during a transcontinental flight or a long day of convention meetings than watching your laptop battery drop to 12% with four hours of work remaining and no working AC outlet in sight. Portable power banks have undergone a massive technological revolution over the past two years: slow 5-volt USB-A bricks that took eight hours to recharge a smartphone have been replaced by ultra-fast USB-C Power Delivery (PD 3.0 and PD 3.1) stations capable of pumping up to 140 watts directly into a 16-inch MacBook Pro or Dell XPS. Meanwhile, the arrival of Qi2 magnetic wireless charging brings authentic 15-watt snap-on wireless speeds to mobile devices without messy dangling cables.

              Essential Buying Criteria: What Really Matters in a Power Bank

              1. Capacity: Understanding mAh vs. Watt-Hours (Wh) & Airline Limits

              Battery packaging prominently displays milliamp-hours (mAh), typically calculated at the lithium cell's baseline 3.7V nominal voltage. However, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and international airline security agencies (TSA, ICAO) regulate batteries strictly in Watt-hours (Wh). You are legally permitted to carry lithium battery packs up to 100Wh in carry-on luggage without airline approval (and up to 160Wh with airline permission). To calculate Watt-hours: multiply mAh by 3.7 and divide by 1,000. For example: 25,600 mAh × 3.7V ÷ 1,000 = 94.72 Wh. Always choose power banks with the exact Wh rating permanently engraved on the exterior casing to avoid security delays at airport gates.

              2. USB-C Power Delivery: PD 3.0 vs. PD 3.1 (EPR)

              Older power banks only output 15W to 18W, which charges a modern phone slowly and will not charge a laptop at all. USB-C Power Delivery 3.0 caps output at 100 watts (20V/5A)—plenty for 13-inch and 14-inch laptops. The newer USB-C PD 3.1 Extended Power Range (EPR) standard increases voltage up to 28V, enabling 140W single-port charging. If you carry a 16-inch MacBook Pro or a high-performance Windows workstation, verify that the power bank explicitly supports 140W PD 3.1 output.

              3. Qi2 Magnetic Wireless vs. Traditional Qi

              Qi2 is the new open wireless standard developed in collaboration with Apple. Unlike older Qi pads that max out at 7.5W on iPhones and slide around on tables, Qi2 incorporates magnetic ring alignment and authentic 15W wireless charging speeds. Keep in mind that wireless charging generates heat and suffers approximately 30% to 35% efficiency loss compared to wired cables; use wireless for convenience, but use USB-C cords for maximum speed and energy yield.

              4. Recharging Speeds: The Input Bottle-Neck

              A 24,000mAh power bank holds an enormous amount of energy. If the battery bank only supports 15W input, recharging it from zero takes over seven hours. Flagship models (like the Anker 737 or Baseus Blade) support 100W to 140W fast input, allowing you to fully replenish a giant 24,000mAh pack in under an hour while taking a shower before catching your next flight.

              Frequently Asked Questions

              What is the maximum power bank capacity allowed on commercial airline flights (TSA rules)?
              Under FAA and TSA regulations, lithium-ion battery packs are strictly prohibited in checked luggage and must be carried in your carry-on bag. You are legally allowed to bring power banks rated up to 100 Watt-hours (Wh) without any airline approval (which corresponds to approximately 27,000mAh at 3.7V). Power banks between 101Wh and 160Wh require special airline carrier permission. Always ensure the Wh rating is permanently engraved on the battery casing to prevent security confiscation.
              What is the difference between USB-C Power Delivery 3.0 and PD 3.1?
              USB-C Power Delivery 3.0 caps power delivery at 100 watts (using standard 20 volts at 5 amps), which is sufficient to power most smartphones, tablets, and 13-inch or 14-inch laptops. USB-C PD 3.1 Extended Power Range (EPR) increases maximum voltage thresholds up to 28V, 36V, and 48V, enabling single-port charging speeds up to 140W and 240W. This higher wattage is essential to charge demanding 16-inch MacBook Pros and workstation laptops at full operating speeds.
              How much energy is lost when using wireless magnetic charging compared to a cable?
              Magnetic inductive wireless charging (including MagSafe and Qi2) suffers roughly 30% to 35% energy loss due to coil resistance and electromagnetic heat dissipation. By contrast, a direct wired USB-C cable typically operates at 85% to 90% energy efficiency. This means a 10,000mAh power bank will yield roughly 1.5 full charges wirelessly, compared to over 2 full phone charges when using a physical cable.
              Can you use a 100W or 140W power bank to charge a small smartphone safely?
              Yes, completely safely. USB-C Power Delivery uses bidirectional digital communication chips: the power bank and the connected device negotiate voltage and amperage before power flows. A 140W power bank will only deliver the exact maximum wattage requested by your phone (e.g. 20W for an iPhone or 25W for a Samsung Galaxy) and will never push excess power or damage internal batteries.
              How do you convert milliamp-hours (mAh) to Watt-hours (Wh) for flight compliance?
              To convert milliamp-hours to Watt-hours, multiply the capacity in mAh by the battery cell's nominal voltage (which is standard 3.7 volts for consumer lithium-ion cells) and divide by 1,000. The mathematical formula is: (mAh × 3.7V) ÷ 1,000 = Watt-hours (Wh). For instance, a standard 20,000mAh battery pack equates to (20,000 × 3.7) ÷ 1,000 = 74 Watt-hours, which safely clears the 100Wh TSA airline carry-on limit.
