<h3>1. Measure the hole, then pick capacity</h3>
<p dir="auto"><strong>0.7 cu ft</strong> fits dorms and tight counters. <strong>~1.2 cu ft</strong> fits most dinner plates without dominating the counter. <strong>1.6 cu ft</strong> helps big bowls and family leftovers — but measure height under upper cabinets before you buy a tall box.</p>

<h3>2. Wattage sets how long frozen food takes</h3>
<p dir="auto">Rough guide: <strong>~700W</strong> is slow on dense frozen meals; <strong>~1000–1100W</strong> is the everyday sweet spot; <strong>~1200W</strong> class units feel snappier if your circuit and budget allow. <ul dir="auto">
<li><strong>Basic presets</strong> — fine if you will set time yourself.</li>
<li><strong>Sensor reheat</strong> — fewer rubbery leftovers when people stop guessing.</li>
<li><strong>Inverter</strong> — fewer “icy center / scorched edge” complaints on soups and defrost, at a higher price.</li>

<h3>4. Noise, beeps, and cleanup</h3>
<p dir="auto">Open-plan apartments punish loud fans and long end-of-cycle beeps. Sticky turntables and hard-to-wipe trays show up often in complaint clusters — check whether the tray lifts out easily before you care about “smart” menus.</p>

<h3>FAQ — How to choose a microwave</h3>
<h4>1. How many watts do I need in a microwave?</h4>
<p>For everyday frozen meals, aim near 1000–1100W. 700W units stay slow on dense food.</p>
<h4>2. Is inverter technology worth the extra money?</h4>
<p>Yes if you reheat soups and defrost often and hate cold centers. No if you only punch in 90 seconds for leftovers.</p>
<h4>3. What size microwave fits a small apartment counter?</h4>
<p>Most small kitchens do well at about 1.2 cu ft. Go 0.7 only when the counter is truly tiny.</p>
<h4>4. Countertop vs over-the-range — which should I buy?</h4>
<p>Buy over-the-range when the cabinet cutout and vent path already exist. Otherwise start countertop.</p>
<h4>5. Do sensor cook buttons actually help?</h4>
<p>They help people who stop guessing cook times. They do not fix a unit that is too small or underpowered.</p>

<h4>Conclusion</h4>
<p>Measure clearance first, pick wattage for frozen food, then decide if sensor or inverter is worth the upcharge. Most readers land on a mid-size ~1100W countertop; pay more for inverter evenness or go tiny only when space forces it.</p>
